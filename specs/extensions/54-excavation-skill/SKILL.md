# SERMON EXCAVATION SKILL

Extract everything useful from a sermon. Organize it. Verify you got it all.
Then hunt for what you missed. The output is a mass of organized raw material —
not a script, not a summary, not a JSON schema. Compression comes later.

## When to use

Any time you need to extract the substance of a teaching text — a sermon from
Ñāṇananda's Nibbāna series, a sutta, a commentary, a lecture transcript — for
later compression into a movie, essay, or practice system. Works on any text
where the goal is to capture everything useful before deciding what matters.

## The three passes

### Pass 1: Full extraction

Read the entire source text. Do not skim. Do not summarize. Extract every:
- **Metaphor** — similes, images, comparisons ("like a lamp going out," "like
  a tree with no name")
- **Argument** — a claim with evidence or reasoning ("nama means name because
  the toddler knows the ball by feel, not by label")
- **Refutation** — a counter-argument answered ("Buddhaghosa says animals have
  it too; he confuses warming up with cooling down")
- **Definition** — a term defined or redefined ("patigha means resistance, not
  repugnance")
- **Scripture citation** — a sutta, verse, or parallel quoted or referenced
- **Cross-reference** — a parallel in another canon (Chinese, Vedic, etc.)
- **Practical instruction** — anything the reader can do ("come back to the
  state of a child," "slow down like a patient")
- **The turn** — the moment the argument shifts or surprises ("the fire
  simile holds the answer to the tetralemma")
- **The line worth keeping** — a sentence so good it survives compression
  ("the depth of the Dhamma has to be seen through lucidity, just as much as
  one sees the bottom of a tank only when the water is lucid")

Organize every extract into the scaffold sections:
1. What nibbāna is
2. How the mechanism works
3. What keeps the mechanism running
4. What the mechanism builds
5. How to see through it
6. What remains

Plus: Refutations (counter-arguments answered), Evidence (scripture +
parallels), Voice (lines worth keeping verbatim).

**Rules for Pass 1:**
- Extract in the source's own words where possible. Quote, don't paraphrase.
- Every extract carries its context: which sutta, which verse, which argument
  it belongs to.
- Don't judge whether something is "important." If it's in the text, extract
  it. Compression comes later.
- Don't skip the "boring" parts — background, history, commentary failures.
  These often contain the best material.
- Note Pali terms AND their plain-English meaning. The Pali layer is for
  the study layer; the plain English is for the movie.

### Pass 2: Verification against source

Re-read the source text. This time, read it **against** your extraction.
Your job is to find what you missed.

Method:
1. Read the source in chunks of 200-300 words.
2. For each chunk, ask: did I extract everything useful from this?
3. Check specific things:
   - Every quoted verse: did I capture it? The exact wording?
   - Every Pali term: did I note its meaning?
   - Every cross-reference: did I record it (Chinese parallel, Vedic source)?
   - Every named person: did I note their role (Vacchagotta the accuser,
     Sopaka the child arahant, Nanda the reluctant monk)?
   - Every "sidebar" or footnote: these often contain comparative material
     the main text skips.
   - Every turn in the argument: did I catch the moment the point shifts?
   - Every line worth keeping: did I capture it verbatim?
4. Add anything you missed to the excavation, in the right section.
5. Note what you found in a "Pass 2 finds" section at the bottom.

**Rules for Pass 2:**
- Be adversarial with yourself. Assume you missed something.
- Check for named entities: suttas, people, places, texts, parallels.
- Check for exact quotes: if the source quotes a verse, capture it exactly.
- Check for context notes: the source may add a scholarly note, a parallel
  reference, or a translation variant in a sidebar.
- If you find 0 misses, re-read more carefully. There's always something.

### Pass 3: Hole hunting

Now actively search for what the excavation is missing — not against the
source, but against the scaffold and the world.

Method:
1. Read your excavation. For each scaffold section, ask:
   - Is this section filled? Or is it thin?
   - Does the argument actually work? Can someone follow it?
   - What would a skeptical reader challenge?
   - What would a beginner not understand?
2. Check the scaffold sections that are empty or thin. Is there material in
   the sermon that belongs there but I filed elsewhere?
3. Check for structural gaps:
   - Does the argument have a missing link? (e.g., if the sermon argues
     "craving ceases" but doesn't explain HOW, that's a hole.)
   - Is there a term used but never defined?
   - Is there a claim without evidence?
4. Check against the broader context:
   - Does this sermon's argument connect to the other sermons? (Note the
     connections.)
   - Are there Analayo parallels that would strengthen or complicate the
     argument? (Note them even if the sermon doesn't mention them.)
   - Are there pratikas (counter-arguments) from other traditions that this
     sermon doesn't address? (Note them for later.)
5. Add a "Pass 3: holes and connections" section to the excavation.

**Rules for Pass 3:**
- You're not checking the source anymore. You're checking the argument.
- Thin sections are not failures — they're honest. Note what's missing.
- Connections to other sermons are gold. Note every one.
- Don't fill holes with invention. Note the hole. Leave it for research.

## Validation gates

The excavation passes ALL of these gates before it's done. Each gate is a
check, not a score. A FAIL means the excavation needs another pass, not that
it's bad.

### Gate 1: Coverage
- Every paragraph of the source has been read.
- Every quoted verse appears in the excavation (exact wording).
- Every Pali term has a plain-English meaning noted.
- Every named person has a one-line role note.
- Every sutta citation is recorded.
- Every cross-reference (Chinese, Vedic, etc.) is recorded.
- PASS = the excavation covers the source at paragraph granularity.
- FAIL = gaps between source paragraphs and excavation sections.

### Gate 2: Organization
- Every extract is in a scaffold section (or explicitly marked "unsorted").
- Every extract has its context (which sutta, which verse, which argument).
- The scaffold sections are not mixed (e.g., a metaphor in "What nibbāna is"
  should not also appear in "What keeps the mechanism running" unless it
  genuinely belongs in both — note the dual membership).
- PASS = every extract is filed and contextualized.
- FAIL = extracts floating without section or context.

### Gate 3: Fidelity
- Quotes are exact (not paraphrased).
- Pali terms are transcribed correctly.
- The source's own emphasis is preserved (if the source stresses a point,
  the excavation notes the stress).
- No invented content: everything in the excavation is traceable to the
  source text.
- PASS = the excavation could be audited against the source line by line.
- FAIL = paraphrases masquerading as quotes, or invented connections.

### Gate 4: Argument integrity
- The scaffold sections, taken together, form a coherent argument.
- Each section's claims are supported by the extracts in that section.
- Refutations are filed where they belong (the refutation of "animals have
  it too" belongs in "What nibbāna is," not "How to see through it").
- PASS = someone could reconstruct the argument from the excavation alone.
- FAIL = the argument has gaps or the sections don't connect.

### Gate 5: Utility
- The excavation contains material for a movie: metaphors, images, the line
  worth keeping, visual ideas.
- The excavation contains material for a practice: instructions, direct
  experience tests, the "try it" moments.
- The excavation contains material for an essay: the refutations, the
  evidence, the cross-references.
- PASS = a compressor could build a movie, practice, and essay from this.
- FAIL = the excavation is a summary, not a source of raw material.

### Gate 6: Honesty
- Thin sections are marked thin (not padded with invention).
- Uncertainties are noted ("verify exact wording at draft").
- The source's own uncertainties are preserved (if the source says
  "probably," the excavation says "probably").
- Pass 2 finds and Pass 3 holes are recorded, not hidden.
- PASS = the excavation is honest about what it has and what it lacks.
- FAIL = the excavation pretends to be complete when it isn't.

## Output format

The excavation file follows this structure:

```
# SERMON N — EXCAVATION

Source: [filename, word count, free/verified]

## SECTION 1: WHAT NIBBANA IS
[extracts, each with context]

## SECTION 2: HOW THE MECHANISM WORKS
[extracts]

## SECTION 3: WHAT KEEPS THE MECHANISM RUNNING
[extracts]

## SECTION 4: WHAT THE MECHANISM BUILDS
[extracts]

## SECTION 5: HOW TO SEE THROUGH IT
[extracts]

## SECTION 6: WHAT REMAINS
[extracts]

## REFUTATIONS
[counter-arguments answered, each with its section]

## EVIDENCE
[scripture citations, parallels, cross-references]

## VOICE
[lines worth keeping verbatim — the ones that survive compression]

## PASS 2 FINDS
[what Pass 2 added — items missed in Pass 1]

## PASS 3: HOLES AND CONNECTIONS
[thin sections, missing links, connections to other sermons, Analayo
parallels not in the source, pratikas from other traditions]
```

## Common mistakes (don't do these)

- **Summarizing instead of extracting.** "He discusses the fire simile" is
  a summary. The fire simile in full, with the Vacchagotta dialogue and the
  tetralemma dissolution, is an extraction.
- **Paraphrasing quotes.** If the source says "the depth of the Dhamma has
  to be seen through lucidity," write that, not "depth is seen through
  clarity."
- **Skipping the "background" section.** Nanananda's commentary-failure
  material in Sermon 1 contains the tank-bottom-lucid-water line and the
  four historical reasons. These are not background — they're arguments.
- **Filing everything in one section.** If a metaphor appears in the context
  of "what keeps the mechanism running" but also illustrates "what nibbāna
  is," note the dual membership. Don't force it into one box.
- **Inventing connections in Pass 3.** If you think two sermons connect,
  note the connection. Don't assert it as if the source said it.
- **Hiding misses in Pass 2.** If you find something you missed, add it AND
  note it in Pass 2 finds. The finds are evidence the process works.
- **Being satisfied with 0 misses.** If Pass 2 finds nothing, re-read more
  carefully. There's always a footnote, a parallel, a named person you
  skipped.

## Quality rubric

A good excavation:
- Captures the fire simile in Sermon 1 as a complete argument, not a mention.
- Preserves the Vacchagotta dialogue in full (the accusation, the Buddha's
  answer, the tetralemma dissolution).
- Notes the SA 1020 Chinese parallel for the "name has conquered" verse.
- Captures the toddler and rubber ball as a complete illustration, not a
  reference.
- Preserves the Rādha chain as a sequence, not a list.
- Files the warming-versus-cooling refutation where it belongs.
- Notes the Upanishad cross-references (Svetaśvatara, Maitrī) even though
  the sermon only mentions them in passing.
- Contains at least one "line worth keeping" per scaffold section.
- Pass 2 finds are recorded (even if it's just "confirmed 0 misses" — but
  usually there are misses).
- Pass 3 notes at least one connection to another sermon.
- Thin sections are marked thin.

A bad excavation:
- Summarizes instead of extracting.
- Paraphrases quotes.
- Files everything in one section.
- Skips the "background" material.
- Has no Pass 2 or Pass 3.
- Claims completeness it doesn't have.
